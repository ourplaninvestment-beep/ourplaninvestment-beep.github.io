Money guides for U.S. households

Short, plain-English explainers behind the numbers — the same topics Americans search for every day.

01

How much house can you afford?

Lenders like the 28/36 rule: housing costs under 28% of gross monthly income, and all debts under 36%. On an $85,000 salary that's roughly a $1,980 monthly payment — run it through the mortgage calculator with taxes and insurance included, because those add $400–$700 a month in most states.

02

How federal income tax brackets really work

Earning more never makes your whole paycheck taxed at the higher rate. Only the dollars inside each bracket are taxed at that bracket's rate — which is why your effective rate is always lower than your top (marginal) rate. The tax estimator above shows both side by side.

03

The 401(k) match is free money

Most employers match part of what you contribute — commonly 50% of the first 6% of pay. Contributing at least enough to get the full match beats almost any other use of that money. Try the retirement calculator with and without an extra $100 a month to see the 35-year difference.

04

15-year vs 30-year mortgage

A 15-year loan usually carries a lower rate and builds equity far faster, but the monthly payment is much higher. On a $340,000 loan at 6.6%, switching from 30 to 15 years saves well over $200,000 in interest — at the cost of about $800 more per month.

05

What is a healthy BMI?

For adults, the CDC ranges are: underweight below 18.5, healthy 18.5–24.9, overweight 25–29.9, and obese 30 and above. BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis — muscle, age and build all matter, so treat the number as a starting point for a conversation with your doctor.

06

Tipping norms in the USA

Visitors are often surprised: tipping is a core part of pay for U.S. service workers. Plan on 18–20% at sit-down restaurants, $1–$2 per drink at bars, 10–15% for delivery, and a few dollars per night for hotel housekeeping.

07

Why minimum credit card payments keep you in debt

Minimum payments are usually just 1–3% of your balance, so they shrink every month while interest keeps charging on what's left. A $5,000 balance at 22.9% APR paid at a fixed $200 a month is gone in about 32 months with roughly $1,370 of interest. Pay only the minimum and the same debt can drag past a decade and cost far more. Fix the payment, not the minimum — try it in the payoff calculator.

08

The 30% rule for rent, explained

Housing experts and most landlords use 30% of gross monthly income as the rent ceiling. On $60,000 a year that's $1,500 a month. In expensive metros many renters stretch to 35–40%, but then savings, debts and emergencies get squeezed — the rent calculator shows a comfortable range below the ceiling so you can see both numbers before signing a lease.

09

Sales tax: why the price tag is never the price

Unlike most countries, U.S. shelf prices exclude tax. Five states charge no statewide sales tax at all — Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire, Delaware and Alaska (some Alaska towns charge local tax) — while California's 7.25% base is the highest state rate, and combined state-plus-local rates top 10% in parts of Louisiana, Tennessee, Arkansas and Washington. Check the full state table below before a big purchase.

10

Build a starter emergency fund

Three to six months of essential expenses is the standard target, but the first $1,000 matters most — it keeps a car repair or medical bill off a 25% APR credit card. Park it in a high-yield savings account, then use the compound interest calculator to see what steady monthly deposits turn into once the fund is full and the same habit moves to investing.

Sales tax by state 2025

Statewide base sales tax rates for all 50 states and Washington, DC. Your real rate is this base plus city, county and district taxes — in many places the combined rate runs 1–3 points higher. Five states (AK, DE, MT, NH, OR) have no statewide tax; Alaska still allows local taxes.

Rates shown are statewide base rates in effect for 2025, for general merchandise. Groceries, clothing and medicines are taxed differently in many states — some exempt them entirely. Use the sales tax calculator above to total any purchase.

Frequently asked questions

Quick answers about the tools on this page.

Are these calculators really free?

Yes. Every tool on this page is free with no account, no email and no limits. The site is supported by the advertising slots you see on the page.

Is my financial information stored or shared?

No. All calculations run entirely in your own browser using JavaScript. The numbers you type never leave your device and are never sent to a server.

How accurate is the tax estimator?

It applies the official 2025 IRS federal brackets and standard deductions for single, married-filing-jointly and head-of-household filers. It does not include state taxes, credits, or itemized deductions, so use it for planning — not for filing.

Does the mortgage calculator include taxes and insurance?

Yes. Property tax, homeowners insurance and HOA dues are added to principal and interest to give you the full monthly PITI payment most lenders quote.

Can I use these tools on my phone?

Absolutely — every calculator is designed for phones first and works the same on tablets and desktops.

How much rent can I afford on my salary?

A common rule is to keep rent at or below 30% of your gross monthly income. On a $60,000 salary that is about $1,500 a month. The rent affordability calculator shows a comfortable, standard and maximum range for your income.

How long will it take to pay off my credit card?

It depends on your balance, APR and monthly payment. Paying only the minimum can stretch a $5,000 balance at 22% APR beyond 10 years, while a fixed $200 monthly payment clears it in about 32 months. The credit card payoff calculator shows your exact timeline and total interest.

What is the sales tax rate in my state?

Statewide base rates for 2025 range from 0% in Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire and Delaware to 7.25% in California and 7% in Tennessee, Indiana, Mississippi and Rhode Island. Cities and counties add local tax on top, so your combined rate is usually higher than the state base rate — see the full table above.

How do I calculate a percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new value, divide by the old value, then multiply by 100. For example, going from $50 to $65 is a 30% increase. The percentage calculator does all three common percentage jobs instantly.
